Hundred Quarters 百刻

2026 · Digital Art · Landscape Simulation · Computer Vision

Output
  • 百刻 Hundred Quarters — YouTube channel, one scroll per solar term and per 候 (five-day season)
Tools
  • Python, NumPy / CuPy — the scroll renderer, on the GPU
  • NVIDIA Warp — wind and plant stems as physical rods
  • Faust / DawDreamer — generative process music, tuned to the season
  • Depth Anything V2 — monocular depth, to measure the scroll’s space

Hundred Quarters is a landscape that runs as a program. Each video is one continuous handscroll, drawn frame by frame from a score: the land and water, the hills, the plants and the animals are placed by a planner, painted as flat cut paper, and carried past a camera through one day’s light. Nothing is filmed and no image is generated by a model; every scene is composed, and every rule is written.

The rules come from the classical calendar and its texts. The year is cut into the twenty-four solar terms and seventy-two five-day seasons (候), each with its own mode and key after the 《月令》, its own palette, and the plant or animal its season names. Durations come from the time units of the 摩诃僧祇律: a breath (息) of 7.2 seconds, a quarter (刻) of 14:24, a double hour of exactly 2:00:00. The sound is process music built on that breath, and it shares the score’s timeline with the picture, so the scroll reaches its shore as the bell rings.

The research question is space. A Chinese handscroll makes depth out of 留白 and mist between flat planes, with no perspective floor. A video scroll has to move, and motion asks how far each plane is. The project reads its own frames with a monocular depth model, compares them with photographs of the karst landscapes they derive from, and rebuilds the renderer around one real camera, so the flat planes keep their painterly look while they pass at the rates a lens would see.

The latest piece. Every video on the channel goes public at the moment its season begins.

The frame, far to near

One frame of the two-hour study piece, built up in the order the renderer lays it. Each plane is drawn flat; its distance decides only its size and how fast it passes.

The same frame taken apart: each plane alone, far at the left, with its distance from the camera and its pace across the screen.

One camera

A level pinhole camera 0.58 m above the ground, its horizon at 0.795 of the frame, a 12° vertical field (about a 114 mm lens): a cat’s eye from far off. On the ground a thing’s distance follows from its foot alone, Z = h f / (y − yhorizon); everything passes at 1 / Z. The cat, 0.30 m tall, walks 34.5 m off; the far hills stand 3 km away and drift a hundredth as fast.

Reading depth with a model

The finished frame (left), the depth the renderer itself knows, each plane at its camera distance (centre), and Depth Anything V2 reading the same frame (right). The model finds the near grass, the cat and the ground plane, and sees the hills as cut-outs a little in front of the sky: it reads the paper as paper.

Frame · engine depth (each layer at its camera distance) · Depth Anything V2, relative.

The same model on a frame before the camera was fixed. Its readings, with optical flow between frames, showed the planes passing at hand-set rates that no single lens could give, and the plants drawn at a scale twelve times smaller than the cat’s. That finding set the one camera above.
