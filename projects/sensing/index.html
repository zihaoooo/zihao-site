Urban Sensing

2016–Ongoing · Physical Computing

Collaborators
  • Mona El Khafif — University of Virginia
  • Andrew Mondschein — University of Virginia
  • Eric Field — University of Virginia

An ongoing line of work rather than a single project: over the years I have built DIY sensing devices to engage urban environmental data, and responsive installations that give that data a physical form.

The sensing kits are open-hardware assemblies — an Arduino, a GPS breakout, a microSD data logger, and a stack of environmental sensors for light level, color temperature, sound, and CO₂ — housed in a palm-sized enclosure that a person can carry while walking, biking, or driving. Because the kit is cheap and legible, the act of measuring becomes something a resident can do, rather than something done to a neighborhood.

The devices fed Community Centered Urban Sensing, a project in Charlottesville, Virginia that paired volunteer-carried kits with a web platform. Participants walked their own neighborhoods, and the tracks they produced were aggregated into maps of light, sound, and air quality alongside geotagged photographs and comments. A dark stretch of sidewalk registers both as five lux and as “I don't feel safe even on my bike” — the platform holds the measurement and the testimony together, at the same point on the map.

The second strand is responsive design: fiber-optic and LED objects driven by the same sensor logic, where environmental readings become light, movement, and color in a room. The instrument and the installation are two ways of asking the same question — what does it feel like to inhabit data that is usually invisible?
