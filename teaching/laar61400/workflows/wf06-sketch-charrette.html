Partner Talk
A charrette is a short, intense working session on one design problem. Each of you designs by hand, fast, with no screens. I walk around, demo at your desk, and draw on trace over your ideas.
Software makes a polished image in seconds. Thinking a design through on paper, at the right scale, in front of a client or a jury, is still yours to do. These two sessions train it.
Session 1 Technique + Warm-Up
01 Bring Your Kit Before class
Everything fits in a bag. Bring it to both sessions.
  • 11"×17" trace pad, enough for six sheets
  • Two pen weights: a fine liner (Micron 03 or 05) and a Sharpie or brush pen
  • An architect's or engineer's scale with 1"=10' and 1"=20'
  • Tape and your sketchbook
02 Demo: Fast Graphics In class
Watch the demo and draw along on trace.
  • Line weight sets hierarchy. Heavy for cut lines and building edges, light for paving and texture.
  • Plan graphics. Trees as circles with a shadow, seating as solid edges, paving as a few joints.
  • Fast sections. Ground line first, then the body, then what shelters the body.
  • One-point perspective. Horizon at eye height, about 5' above the ground.
  • Tone by hatching. Shade with the pen alone: parallel strokes for shadow, closer together for darker, crossed for the darkest.
03 Warm-Up: A Bench and a Tree 2 hours
On one 11"×17" sheet, design a corner of about 30'×30' where one person can sit in the shade of one tree. Draw it at 1"=10': a plan, a section through the seat, and a quick perspective. Put a person in every drawing.
  • At 1"=10' a standing person is about 0.6" tall. Measure it with your scale.
  • Seat height 18", backrest 32", tree canopy 12' or higher.
Before Session 2, read the brief in Step 04 and print the base plan.
Session 2 Studio Charrette
04 The Site and a First Idea Before class
The site is a planted triangle where a diagonal avenue cuts across the Manhattan grid in Harlem, about 115 by 180 feet curb to curb. Design it as a public space for the people who live, wait, and pass through here. Treat the base plan as your site; the real one is a starting point, not a limit.
  • Edges. A wide two-way boulevard with a planted median and a bike lane runs along one side. A one-way avenue with angled parking on both sides runs along the diagonal. A quieter cross street closes the third side.
  • Context. Five- and six-story apartment buildings with storefronts at street level face the triangle. Churches, a public library, small restaurants, and a subway station are within a block or two. A bus stop sits on the boulevard side, near the tip.
  • The street is yours to decide. NYC's Open Streets program lets neighborhoods close streets to cars. You decide what happens to the one-way avenue and its parking.
  • Heat. Harlem is among the most heat-vulnerable parts of the city. Design for shade, cooling, and a place to rest on the hottest day of the year.
  • Existing trees. The base plan shows the existing trees with their approximate canopies. Decide which ones your design keeps and builds around.
  • Challenges. Traffic on three sides, long crossings at the boulevard, the narrow tip of the triangle, and people waiting for the bus beside fast boulevard traffic.
Print the base plan at 100% on 11×17 and draw on trace over it. Come to Session 2 with one sentence naming your idea and a thumbnail sketch of it.
Download base plan (PDF, 11×17 at 1"=20')↓
05 Develop the Design In class
Work on all the drawings at once and move between them as the idea needs. A section often changes the plan; a perspective shows what the plan missed. I come by your desk to demo and to sketch on trace over your sheets. Keep that trace.
  • Plan, 1"=20'. Trace over the base plan, the scale of your WF01 drafting. Mark the section cut lines and add a small concept diagram.
  • Two sections, 1"=10'. At least six people in each, each doing something different: sitting, leaning, walking, waiting for a bus.
  • Eye-level perspective. Stand somewhere in your plaza and draw what you see. Add a title and your name.
06 Talk It Through with a Partner Last 20 min
Pair up and walk your partner through your design, about five minutes each, using your drawings: the idea, how a person moves through and uses the space, and what you would develop next. Then switch.
07 Finish and Submit Due Nov 19
Finish the three sheets by hand. Photograph each one flat in even light, along with the trace from our desk sessions, combine them into one PDF, and post it to Miro.
How It Is Graded
  • Idea — one clear design idea you can read from the drawings
  • Scale — bodies, seats, steps, and trees sized correctly
  • Graphics — line weight and hatching that set a clear hierarchy
  • Development — how the idea grew across the drawings and the desk sessions
