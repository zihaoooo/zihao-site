Six things every field has already settled

  • What is the object? The gene, the market, the text, the site, the population, the artifact.
  • What counts as evidence? A measurement, a transcript, a document, a specimen, a drawing, a benchmark.
  • What is a finding? An effect size. A reading. A mechanism. A working prototype. A reframing.
  • How much error is tolerable, and what has to be reported about it?
  • Where does the work go? A journal, a monograph, a conference, a building, an exhibition, a repository.
  • Who is allowed to judge it? Peers, a committee, a client, a jury, the public, a court.
  • You know your own field’s six answers. You have probably never written them down.

The differences are not arbitrary

  • It is tempting to treat disciplinary differences as tribal habits. Mostly they are adaptations to the object.
  • You cannot randomize the French Revolution. There was one, it already happened, and there is no control group.
  • You cannot interview a protein about its experience, and a survey of proteins would not mean anything.
  • So: where you can intervene and repeat, you get experiment. Where you cannot, you get comparison, interpretation, and the careful reading of what survives.
  • Where the object is something that does not exist yet, you get design — and the evidence is the thing you built.
  • Different equipment for different quarry. Not different amounts of rigor.

What every one of them shares

  • A question that could have gone another way. If the answer was fixed in advance, no field counts it.
  • Evidence somebody else could go and check. The form varies enormously; the obligation does not.
  • An argument, exposed to people qualified to attack it. Peer review, committee, jury, crit, court, replication — different rooms, same function.
  • What does not travel: sample sizes, what counts as proof, whether the author may appear in the text, how much interpretation is allowed, and how long a piece of work should take.
  • When you are out of your depth in another field, hold onto the three that travel and ask about the rest.

How to read a paper from a field you were not trained in

  • Find the claim first. Usually the last paragraph of the introduction. Read that before anything else.
  • Read the methods for what it is defending against. Every methods section is an argument with anticipated objections. The objections tell you the field’s standards.
  • Look at the citations’ dates. A field citing mostly the last five years moves fast and forgets; one citing across a century is in a long argument.
  • Ask what the field would call a devastating criticism — then check whether you are about to make it, and whether it is devastating there.
  • Do not judge the prose. Density in a humanities article and terseness in a physics one are both local conventions, not evidence of anything.
  • Ask someone in that field to tell you what is good. Ten minutes saves you a month.

False friends — words that betray you

  • Model — a physical object, a statistical specification, a simulation, or a simplified theory. Four fields, four meanings.
  • Significant — passes a statistical threshold, or matters. These come apart constantly, in both directions.
  • Critical — essential, or working in a tradition that analyzes power. A “critical study” means opposite things two doors apart.
  • Bias — a systematic error to be corrected, or an unavoidable position to be declared.
  • Theory — the most substantiated explanation available, or a system of ideas to think with.
  • Field — a discipline, a site you go to, or a physical continuum. Sample — a statistical draw, or a physical specimen.
  • When a meeting keeps snagging on one word, stop and define it. That word is doing all the damage.

Multidisciplinary, interdisciplinary, transdisciplinary

What happensWhat integratesTell-tale output
Multi­disciplinarySeveral fields work the same problem in parallel, each from its own side.Nothing, really. The findings sit next to each other.An edited volume where each chapter could stand alone.
Inter­disciplinaryMethods and concepts are actually combined into something neither field had.The framework itself. Somebody’s method changed.A paper that neither field could have produced, and both can review.
Trans­disciplinaryAcademics work with people outside the academy as knowledge partners, not subjects.The problem definition — set with the partners, not for them.Knowledge that has to hold up outside the university too.
Most projects that call themselves interdisciplinary are multidisciplinary. The test is simple: did anyone’s method actually change?

Why interdisciplinary work is hard

  • The disagreements are epistemological, and they arrive in bundles. What counts as a fact, what a causal claim requires, whether values belong in the analysis — these reinforce each other, so you cannot settle one in isolation.
  • Disciplinary capture. The field with the numbers, the money, or the louder methodological confidence quietly sets the terms, and the other side’s contribution becomes context.
  • Time. Collaborators are held together by a project deadline, not a shared community — so there is always more incentive to finish than to resolve a deep disagreement.
  • The institutions are built against it. Separate journals and review standards, funding panels organized by field, hiring and tenure lines that ask which discipline you are in, and reviewers who find the work shallow by their own field’s standards.
  • None of this is a reason not to do it. It is a reason to plan for it.

How to actually work across a boundary

  • Build the shared object first. A map, a dataset, a prototype, a timeline. A boundary object is sturdy enough to be shared and loose enough that each side reads its own meaning into it.
  • Do not begin by agreeing on epistemology. Teams that try never reach the work. The vocabulary grows around the object — jargon, then a working pidgin.
  • Ask the cheap question early: “What would count as a convincing result for you?” Then: “What would make you dismiss this?”
  • Write down the false friends as you find them. A one-page shared glossary prevents months of argument.
  • Decide early where it will be published, because that decides whose standards the work has to satisfy. Deciding it late is how good collaborations end badly.
  • Aim for interactional expertise — enough fluency to ask a real question of the other side, not enough to do their job. That is the achievable target.

Two terms you will meet in funding calls

  • Mode 2 knowledge production Gibbons, Limoges, Nowotny et al., 1994
    • Mode 1 is disciplinary, university-based, judged by peers within a field.
    • Mode 2 is produced in the context of application, by transient teams from many places, and judged partly on whether it works outside the academy. Nowotny calls the result socially robust knowledge.
  • Wicked problems Rittel and Webber, 1973
    • A tame problem can be stated, solved, and checked. A wicked one cannot even be definitively formulated — how you state it already contains the solution you prefer.
    • No stopping rule; solutions are better or worse, never true or false; every attempt changes the situation, so you never get a clean second try.
    • Rittel and Webber were writing about planning, which is why this vocabulary is native to design.
  • Both terms are used loosely in proposals. Using them precisely is a small, cheap way to be taken seriously.

Learn enough of the next field to ask it a real question.

Not enough to do its job. That is the achievable target, and it is usually enough.

Before next week

  • Write your field’s six answers: object, evidence, finding, tolerable error, where it goes, who judges it.
  • Pick the field closest to your project that is not yours. Read one well-cited paper in it and write a paragraph on what its methods section is defending against.
  • Find two false friends between that field and yours. Write both definitions of each.
  • Interview someone from that field for fifteen minutes. Ask only: what would count as a convincing result for you, and what would make you dismiss a study out of hand?
  • Then say, in one sentence, whether your project is multi-, inter-, or transdisciplinary — and be honest about it.
