What is Research

  • The pursuit of new knowledge through the process of discovery. Purdue University
  • Two grammars of the word: Open English Wordnet
    • Noun — a search for knowledge. Verb — to find out systematically.
  • The systematic, intentional process of asking questions, gathering evidence, interpreting data, and generating knowledge. ChatGPT
  • All three agree on two things: knowledge that did not exist before, by a process someone else could follow.

How to do research?

  • What do you want to study? Be specific. You cannot just say you want to study pumpkins — what about pumpkins?
  • Why, and why now? What is at stake — climate, injustice, public health, democracy?
  • How will you study it? That is your method.
  • What is the outcome? A paper, a dataset, a model, an exhibition, a policy brief. Say which.

Research is never neutral

From a topic to a question

  • A topic is a noun. A question is something evidence can answer.
  • Four dials: place, time, population, comparison. Most people turn only the first.
  • Pumpkins → pumpkin farming in the Northeast → how has it changed since agritourism became its main market?
  • Stop when you can picture the evidence. You can also over-narrow.

Ask a how question

  • Why asks for a cause you would have to isolate from all the others. What stops at description. How names a process — and a process has parts you can go look at.
  • Why did the neighborhood gentrify? → How did rezoning, lending and marketing work on each other?
  • Why is the river polluted? → How does permitting distribute discharge, and who is downstream of whom?
  • Keep the why. It is your significance section, not your question.

What each stem commits you to

StemYou are promising
What is…A description or a map.
How does…A process or mechanism.
Why does…A cause.
Under what conditions…A boundary on a known claim.

Is it researchable?

  • Answerable with evidence · arguable · scoped · reachable in the time you have.
  • It fills a gap. “Nobody has studied this” usually means you have not found them yet.
  • It fails when it is too broad, already answered, smuggles the answer, or is two questions in a trench coat.

What is a Method

  • Quantitative and qualitative are how scientists traditionally split it — numbers and statistical analysis, against words, images and meaning.
  • A starting point, not a cage. Also: historical, experimental, computational, theoretical, practice-based.
  • A method is simply how you study something. State it so specifically that someone else could repeat it.
  • Combine two carefully and you have a new one.

What counts as evidence

  • Every field has a settlement about what counts: a convention, not a law of nature.
  • Ask of any source: where did it come from? What does it leave out? How far can it carry?

Name your method with verbs

  • Verb + object + purpose: I will [do this] to [this material] in order to find out [that].
  • Compare county records across four decades to find when the market shifted. Interview twelve growers to understand how they decide what to plant.
  • If you cannot write that sentence, you have a topic and a hope, not a method.
  • Careful with “explore.” The work should sound exploratory, not like a fishing trip — and the fix is a specific method, not a more confident adjective.
  • Post-rationalization is allowed. It should also be fun.

What theory is, and is not

  • A well-substantiated explanation that can incorporate laws, hypotheses and facts. AMNH  ·  A system of ideas intended to explain something. Oxford
  • Theory bridges, mediates and reconciles thought and action. Beth Meyer
  • Not a guess — the academic sense is the opposite of “just a theory.” Not decoration — a name in your introduction that never touches your evidence is a citation, not a framework.
  • Not optional. Refusing theory gets you whatever assumptions your training installed, running unexamined.

The stack beneath your method

  • Ontology — what you think exists.
  • Epistemology — how you think it can be known.
  • Methodology — why your methods suit that.
  • Method — the thing you actually do on Tuesday.
  • Everyone has all four. Most people only choose the last, and inherit the rest from their training without noticing.

Four positions you will meet

Reality is…So knowledge comes from…
PositivismOut there, single, independent of us.Measurement and control; the researcher stays out of it.
InterpretivismMade by people, plural, situated.Understanding meaning from the inside; the researcher is part of the instrument.
Critical realismReal, but reached only through our concepts.Inferring the mechanisms under what we observe.
Critical theoryReal, and shaped by power.Analysis that takes a side and says so.
Find your discipline. Then find the one next door. That gap is where the arguments live.

Four things you can do with a theory

  • Apply it to a case it has not met · test it where it makes a prediction · extend it · contest it.
  • Contesting is the highest-value move. You may contest a famous theory with a small case — that is how most got revised.
  • It goes wrong as decoration, as an alibi for gathering no evidence, when it is so strong that every case confirms it, and as framework shopping.
  • Name what your framework cannot see. That reads as command of the material.

Paradigms: science grows by breaking and rebuilding

  • Normal science is puzzle-solving inside an agreed framework. It is where the precision comes from, and it is what you will be doing.
  • An anomaly is a result that will not go away. People patch first; when the patches stop convincing, that is crisis.
  • A paradigm is learned by doing its problem sets, which is why it is hard to state and hard to leave. Thomas Kuhn, 1962

Incommensurability: no common measure, still comparable

  • Like a square’s diagonal and side: no shared unit, but you can still say which is longer.
  • Rival paradigms differ on what the question is, what counts as evidence, and what a good answer looks like.
  • Not “anything goes” and not unintelligible: you can learn the other paradigm like a language. Choice stays rational on shared values, not rules (accurate, consistent, broad, simple, fruitful).

Part VII

Research is never neutral

A discipline is not a subject. It is a settlement about what counts.

That is why the field next door can be rigorous and still disagree with you about everything.

What every field has already settled

  • What is the object? What counts as evidence? What is a finding? How much error is tolerable? Where does it go, and who judges it?
  • Three things travel everywhere: a question that could have gone another way, evidence someone else could check, an argument exposed to people qualified to attack it.
  • Peer review, committee, crit, court and replication are different rooms for the same function.

Different equipment, not different rigor

Sciences & engineeringSocial sciencesHumanitiesDesign & the arts
MoveIsolate, intervene, repeat; benchmark.Sample and infer, or go deep on few cases.Read closely, situate, argue.Make it, in order to find out.
EvidenceMeasurements with stated uncertainty.Surveys, records, transcripts, fieldnotes.The source itself.The drawing, model, prototype, site.
A findingAn effect that survives replication.An association or a mechanism.A better reading than the received one.A proposition made arguable.
You cannot randomize the French Revolution. Method follows object.

False friends

  • Model — an object, a statistical specification, a simulation, or a simplified theory.
  • Significant — passes a threshold, or matters. Bias — an error to correct, or a position to declare.
  • Critical — essential, or analyzing power. Theory, field and sample all split the same way.
  • When a meeting keeps snagging on one word, stop and define it. That word is doing all the damage.

Multi, inter, trans

What happensWhat integrates
Multi­disciplinarySeveral fields work the same problem in parallel.Nothing. The findings sit next to each other.
Inter­disciplinaryMethods and concepts combine into something neither field had.The framework. Somebody’s method changed.
Trans­disciplinaryPartners outside the academy join as knowledge partners.The problem definition, set with them.
Most projects calling themselves interdisciplinary are multidisciplinary. The test: did anyone’s method actually change?

Why working across is hard

  • The disagreements are epistemological, and they arrive in self-reinforcing bundles — you cannot settle one in isolation.
  • Disciplinary capture: the side with the numbers or the money quietly sets the terms.
  • The institutions are built against it — separate journals, review standards, panels, tenure lines.
  • Unnamed, the disagreement resurfaces later as a personality problem.

“Feminist objectivity means quite simply situated knowledges.”

Donna Haraway, “Situated Knowledges,” 1988.

State where you are asking from. Every dataset records what someone chose to measure. Rigor lets others check you: what would convince you that you are wrong?

Before next week

  • Your project in one sentence: I will [verb] [material] to find out [what], because [who cares]. Make it a how.
  • Name the one dataset or archive it depends on, and confirm you can get it.
  • Name the framework you are already using, and what it cannot see.
  • Find two false friends between your field and the one closest to your project.
