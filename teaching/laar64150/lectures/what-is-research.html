What is Research

  • Research is the pursuit of new knowledge through the process of discovery. Purdue University
  • Two grammars of the word: Open English Wordnet
    • Noun: enquiry, inquiry, a search for knowledge
    • Verb: attempt to find out in a systematic and scientific manner
  • Research is the systematic and intentional process of asking questions, gathering evidence, interpreting data, and generating knowledge — a way of making sense of the world, whether that world is social, ecological, technological or historical. ChatGPT

Research is a conversation you join, not a container you fill.

Someone has already been arguing about your topic. Your first job is to find out who, and what they disagree about.

How to do research?

  • What do you want to study?
    • Be specific. You cannot just say you want to study pumpkins — what about pumpkins?
  • Why do you want to study it, and why now?
    • What is at stake in the topic — climate change, social injustice, public health, democracy?
  • How are you going to study it? What is your method?
    • How do you collect, analyze and interpret information? Quantitative, qualitative, historical, experimental, computational?
  • What is your outcome?
    • A paper, a dataset, a model, an exhibition, a policy brief, a piece of software — say which.

Writing about your research

From a topic to a question

  • A topic is a noun. A question is something a piece of evidence can answer.
  • There are four dials, and most people only turn the first:
    • Place — a country, a watershed, a corridor, a building, one room.
    • Time — a century, a decade, a season, the week of the storm.
    • Population or object — which people, which documents, which species, which files.
    • Comparison — against what? Before and after, here and there. The cheapest way to turn a description into an argument.
  • Turn them one at a time:
    • Pumpkins → too big to fail at.
    • Pumpkin farming in the Northeast → a place, but still no question.
    • How has pumpkin farming in the Northeast changed since agritourism became its main market? → now there is something to find out.
  • Stop when you can picture the evidence — the actual folder, dataset or list of names. You can also over-narrow: one block in one month has nobody to talk to.

Ask a how question

  • When a project is stuck, the fix is nearly always the same: turn the question into a how.
  • Why asks for a cause. To answer it you have to rule out the other causes — which one researcher, in one semester, with one case, cannot do. So the answers come out either obvious or unprovable.
  • What asks for a description. You will get one, and then you will be sitting on a description wondering what the argument was.
  • How asks for a process — a sequence, a mechanism, an unfolding. A process has parts, and the parts are things you can go and look at.
  • Keep the why. It is your significance section, not your question.

Converting a why into a how

  • Why did the neighborhood gentrify?
    • → How did rezoning, lending and marketing work on each other to change who could afford to stay?
  • Why is the river polluted?
    • → How does the permitting system distribute discharge along the river, and who ends up downstream of whom?
  • Why don’t residents trust the agency?
    • → How do residents describe their encounters with the agency, and where in those accounts does the trust break?
  • Notice what happened: each rewrite named a process with parts, and each part is a place to go collect something.

What each question stem commits you to

StemYou are promisingEvidence it needsIts typical trap
What is / are…A description or a map.A sample, a survey, a catalog.Stops at description and never argues.
How does / did…A process or mechanism.A sequence you can trace: records, accounts, steps.Too many moving parts; bound it.
Why does…A cause.Comparison and control; ruling out rivals.Unanswerable at one project’s scale — unless your design can isolate.
Under what conditions…A boundary on a known claim.Cases that differ on one thing.Needs real contrast cases, not examples.

Experimental work asks “what is the effect of” and answers it, because the design does the ruling-out. Match the stem to the evidence you can get.

What makes a question researchable

  • Answerable with evidence — not with an opinion, and not only with a definition.
  • Arguable — a reasonable person could answer it differently. If nobody could disagree, it is not a finding.
  • Scoped — bounded in place, time and population, so you can finish it.
  • Reachable — the evidence exists and you can get to it in the time you have. Check access early: archives close, data costs money, permissions take months, people say no.
  • Worth answering — someone other than you should care. Be able to say who.
  • The clinical fields compress this into FINER — Feasible, Interesting, Novel, Ethical, Relevant. Use it as a diagnostic, not a grade: find the weakest letter and fix that one. Hulley et al., Designing Clinical Research
    • It is almost always the F.

Seven ways a question fails

  • Too broad — no place, no time, no population. The dials are all at zero.
  • Too narrow — technically answerable, but nobody outside the case cares, and there is nothing to compare it to.
  • Already answered — and you would know, if you had worked the citation graph.
  • Unanswerable — the evidence does not exist, is sealed, or would take a decade and a team.
  • Smuggles the answer — how can we fix the failure of x? assumes the failure, names the villain, and fixes the finding in advance.
  • Not arguable — it has one obvious answer, or it is really a definition question.
  • Two questions in a trench coat — joined by an “and,” needing two methods, getting neither. Pick one; the other is your next project.

What is a Method

  • Quantitative and qualitative are the traditional way scientists talk about method.
  • The standard split:
    • Quantitative — numerical data, statistical analysis; measurable, expressed in numbers, comparable across cases.
    • Qualitative — non-numerical data (words, images, sounds); experience, meaning and attitude, via interview, observation, close reading.
  • The split is a starting point, not a cage. Plenty of good work is neither, or both:
    • Historical — reconstructing what happened from records that survive.
    • Experimental — changing one thing on purpose to see what follows.
    • Computational — modelling, simulating, or mining a corpus too large to read.
    • Theoretical — using a framework to reinterpret what is already known.
    • Practice-based — making, building or testing something as the inquiry itself.
  • A method is simply how you study something. State it so specifically that someone else could repeat it.
  • Combine two methods carefully and you have a new one.

What counts as evidence

  • Every field has a settlement about what counts — a measurement, a transcript, a document, an artifact, a simulation run, a drawing.
  • That settlement is a convention, not a law of nature. It was argued into place and it changes.
  • Three questions to ask of any piece of evidence you use:
    • Where did it come from? Who produced it, for what purpose, and who paid?
    • What does it leave out? Every record is also a record of what was not recorded.
    • How far can it carry? One case supports a claim about one case.
  • Say out loud which standard you are working to, and why it fits your question.

Methods are made things.

A model, a drawing, a corpus, a map — researchers build instruments in order to think with them.

Name your method with verbs

  • A method statement is verb + object + purpose: I will [do this] to [this material] in order to [find out that].
    • Compare county crop records across four decades to identify when the market shifted.
    • Interview twelve growers to understand how they decide what to plant.
    • Reconstruct a nineteenth-century supply chain from shipping manifests to trace where the seed came from.
    • Simulate the watershed under three rainfall scenarios to bound the flood risk.
  • If you cannot write that sentence, you have a topic and a hope, not a method.
  • Post-rationalization is allowed. You will often name what you were doing only after you did it — then go back and write it down properly.
  • It should be fun. A question you are not curious about will not survive the boring middle of the project.

Theorizing as a Method

  • What is theory?
    • A well-substantiated explanation of an aspect of the natural world that can incorporate laws, hypotheses and facts. American Museum of Natural History
    • A supposition or system of ideas intended to explain something, especially one based on general principles independent of the thing to be explained. Oxford Dictionary
  • Why do we need theory?
    • Theories provide frameworks and vocabularies to study with; they supply the language to create discourse and generate knowledge within.
    • A framework also tells you what to look at — which is why choosing one is a decision with consequences, not a formality.
  • Theory bridges, mediates and reconciles thought and action. Beth Meyer

Four things theory is not

  • Not a guess. The everyday “it is just a theory” is the opposite of the academic sense, where a theory is the most substantiated thing a field has.
  • Not a decoration. A famous name in your introduction that never touches your evidence again is not a framework. It is a citation.
  • Not the opposite of practice. Every practice runs on assumptions about how the world works. Theory is what those assumptions look like when written down.
  • Not optional. Refusing theory does not get you a view from nowhere — it gets you whatever assumptions your training installed, running unexamined.

The stack beneath your method

  • Ontology — what you think exists. Is “a neighborhood” a real thing with properties, or a name people give to a pattern of behavior?
  • Epistemology — how you think it can be known. By measuring it? By asking the people in it? By reading what was written about it?
  • Methodology — the reasoned argument for why your methods suit that. The theory of your methods.
  • Method — the actual thing you do on Tuesday. The survey, the interview, the excavation, the model run.
  • Everyone has all four. Most people only ever choose the last one, and inherit the other three from their training without noticing.
  • A proposal is stronger when the stack lines up — and incoherent when it does not.

Three positions you will meet

Reality is…So knowledge comes from…Typical methods
PositivismOut there, single, independent of us.Measurement, control, replication. The researcher stays out of it.Experiment, survey, statistical inference.
InterpretivismMade and remade by people, plural, situated.Understanding meaning from the inside; the researcher is part of the instrument.Interview, ethnography, close reading.

Find your discipline on this table. Then find the discipline next door. That gap is where most interdisciplinary arguments actually live.

Theory as a lens — one object, three projects

  • Take the pumpkin. Change the framework and you change the finding:
    • Economic — a commodity in a seasonal market; evidence is price series, acreage, labor cost.
    • Ecological — a cultivar in a soil and pollinator system; evidence is yield trials, pollinator counts, disease records.
    • Cultural and colonial — a plant carried across an ocean and folded into a national holiday; evidence is seed catalogs, advertisements, cookbooks, paintings.
  • None of these is the neutral one. The lens is part of the finding.
  • So name your framework in the proposal, and name what it makes you unable to see.

Four things you can do with a theory

  • Apply it. Take the framework to a case it has not been taken to. Lowest risk, and a real contribution if the case is genuinely new.
  • Test it. Set up a case where the theory makes a prediction, and see. Requires the theory to be specific enough to be wrong.
  • Extend it. The theory nearly works; your case shows what has to be added for it to cover more ground.
  • Contest it. Your case is one the theory cannot handle, and you say why. The highest-value move, and the one students are most afraid to make.
  • You are allowed to contest a famous theory with a small case. That is, in fact, how most of them got revised.

Five ways theory goes wrong

  • Decoration — a famous name in the introduction, never seen again. The reader notices.
  • Alibi — theory used to excuse not gathering evidence. “My framework is critical, so I do not need data” is not a position, it is an exit.
  • Crushing — the framework is so strong that every case confirms it. If nothing could have come out otherwise, you ran an illustration, not a study.
  • Wrong grain — a theory of everything applied to a single afternoon, or a theory of one interaction stretched over a century.
  • Framework shopping — a whole semester auditioning theories instead of looking at anything. Pick one, use it on real evidence for two weeks, and you will learn more about whether it fits than from another month of reading.

Writing about your research

Science does not grow by accumulation. It grows by breaking and rebuilding.

Thomas Kuhn, The Structure of Scientific Revolutions, 1962.

Normal science, anomaly, crisis

  • Normal science is puzzle-solving inside an agreed framework — and Kuhn does not mean that as an insult. A puzzle is a problem the framework guarantees has a solution, and that guarantee is what lets someone spend years on a narrow question.
    • The cost: normal science is not trying to falsify its framework. It is trying to extend it and fit more of the world into it.
    • Almost all of your work will be normal science. That is fine. Know that it is what you are doing.
  • An anomaly is a result the framework says should not happen, and which will not go away. Ordinary failures — bad instrument, bad sample — are absorbed, and usually should be. An anomaly persists, appears in more than one lab, and sits where the community cannot route around it.
  • The response is not abandonment but patching: extra assumptions, corrections, special cases. Against Popper, Kuhn says this is not dishonesty. It is how research works.
  • Crisis is when the patches stop convincing people, and practitioners start arguing about foundations again.

“Paradigm” — a word Kuhn half regretted

  • In the 1962 text the word does too many jobs. Margaret Masterman counted twenty-one distinct senses of it, and Kuhn accepted that the criticism was fair.
  • In the 1969 postscript he split it in two:
    • Disciplinary matrix — what a community shares: what it thinks exists, which questions are legitimate, what the methods are, what a good answer looks like, how much error is tolerable.
    • Exemplar — the concrete worked problems students are drilled on. The pendulum, the model organism, the canonical case, the studio project.
  • The exemplar sense is the deeper one. You learn a paradigm by doing its problem sets, not by reading its axioms — which is why it is so hard to state, and so hard to leave.
  • A paradigm is never abandoned into a vacuum. It is replaced. Until a rival exists, a broken framework beats no framework.
  • Late in life Kuhn said he would rather have used neither word than watch “paradigm shift” become a marketing term.

Incommensurability — no common measure

  • The word comes from geometry. The diagonal of a square and its side are incommensurable: no unit measures both a whole number of times. They are still perfectly comparable — you can say which is longer. There is just no common ruler.
  • Kuhn’s claim: rival paradigms are like that. No neutral, framework-free standard that both sides accept could simply decide the contest.
  • Three kinds were in play:
    • Methodological — they disagree about which problems matter, so they cannot agree on the scoreboard.
    • Observational — trained differently, they do not see the same thing. The expert sees a diagnosis where the novice sees a smudge.
    • Semantic or taxonomic — the shared words carve the world differently. “Mass” in Newton and “mass” in Einstein are not interchangeable.
  • Later Kuhn narrowed the thesis to the third — local failures of translation between two taxonomies. That narrower claim is the defensible one, and the one to use.

What it does not mean — and how a choice is still rational

  • Not incomparability. The two can be compared — just not by a neutral algorithm that settles it.
  • Not unintelligibility. You can learn the other paradigm. Kuhn learned Aristotle’s. It takes work, like a language, and that work is the point. Davidson’s objection is sharp: to say two schemes are untranslatable, you must have translated enough to notice.
  • Not “anything goes.” Kuhn denied the relativist reading for thirty years, and he was right to.
  • Kuhn-loss: a new paradigm does not explain everything the old one did. Questions once answerable stop being questions. Revolutions are trades, not pure gains.
  • So how is the choice rational? In 1977 Kuhn named five shared values — accurate, consistent, broad in scope, simple, fruitful — and argued they work as values, not rules: everyone accepts them, they still do not settle the matter, because reasonable people weigh them differently and they conflict (the more accurate theory is often the uglier one).
  • That is not a defect. A field where everyone was compelled to the same conclusion at the same moment could never change its mind.

You are inside one right now

  • Your field trained you into a matrix and a set of exemplars, mostly without stating it — through problem sets, reviews, protocols, and what got a good grade.
  • You can catch it at the edges:
    • When a question strikes you as not a real question — that is the matrix, filtering.
    • When another field’s paper feels sloppy or obvious, and its authors are clearly not stupid.
    • When you cannot say why a piece of work is bad, only that it is.
  • None of that is a failing. It is what competence feels like from the inside. But it means “that is not research” is usually a report about your training.
  • Two ways to misuse this: calling your own project a paradigm shift (reviewers read it as a tell), and using incommensurability as an excuse to stop talking. It is where the conversation starts.

A discipline is not a subject. It is a settlement about what counts.

Somebody argued it into place. That is why the field next door can be rigorous and still disagree with you about everything.

Six things every field has already settled

  • What is the object? The gene, the market, the text, the site, the population, the artifact.
  • What counts as evidence? A measurement, a transcript, a document, a specimen, a drawing, a benchmark.
  • What is a finding? An effect size. A reading. A mechanism. A working prototype. A reframing.
  • How much error is tolerable, and what has to be reported about it?
  • Where does the work go, and who is allowed to judge it — peers, a committee, a client, a jury, a court.
  • Three things do travel everywhere: a question that could have gone another way, evidence someone else could check, and an argument exposed to people qualified to attack it. Peer review, committee, crit, court and replication are different rooms for the same function.
  • What does not travel: sample sizes, what counts as proof, whether the author may appear in the text, and how long a piece of work should take.

False friends — words that betray you

  • Model — a physical object, a statistical specification, a simulation, or a simplified theory. Four fields, four meanings.
  • Significant — passes a statistical threshold, or matters. These come apart constantly, in both directions.
  • Critical — essential, or working in a tradition that analyzes power. A “critical study” means opposite things two doors apart.
  • Bias — a systematic error to be corrected, or an unavoidable position to be declared.
  • Theory — the most substantiated explanation available, or a system of ideas to think with. Field — a discipline, a site, or a physical continuum.
  • When a meeting keeps snagging on one word, stop and define it. That word is doing all the damage.

Most projects that call themselves interdisciplinary are multidisciplinary. The test: did anyone’s method actually change?

Working across a boundary

  • Why it is hard. The disagreements are epistemological and arrive in self-reinforcing bundles — what counts as a fact, what a causal claim requires, whether values belong — so you cannot settle one in isolation. Add disciplinary capture (the side with the numbers or the money quietly sets the terms), deadlines that reward finishing over resolving, and institutions built against it: separate journals, review standards, funding panels and tenure lines.
  • Build the shared object first. A map, a dataset, a prototype, a timeline. Galison calls the arrangement a trading zone: two communities exchanging around a shared object, each keeping its own reasons for caring. A boundary object is sturdy enough to be shared and loose enough that each side reads its own meaning into it.
  • The vocabulary grows around the object — jargon, then a working pidgin. Teams that begin by trying to agree on epistemology never reach the work.
  • Ask early: “What would count as a convincing result for you?” Then: “What would make you dismiss this?” Write down the false friends as you find them.
  • Decide early where it will be published, because that decides whose standards the work has to satisfy.
  • Aim for interactional expertise — enough fluency to ask a real question of the other side, not enough to do their job.

Two terms you will meet in funding calls

  • Mode 2 knowledge production Gibbons, Limoges, Nowotny et al., 1994
    • Mode 1 is disciplinary, university-based, judged by peers within a field.
    • Mode 2 is produced in the context of application, by transient teams from many places, judged partly on whether it works outside the academy. Nowotny calls the result socially robust knowledge.
  • Wicked problems Rittel and Webber, 1973
    • A tame problem can be stated, solved and checked. A wicked one cannot even be definitively formulated — how you state it already contains the solution you prefer.
    • No stopping rule; solutions are better or worse, never true or false; every attempt changes the situation, so there is no clean second try.
  • Both terms get used loosely in proposals. Using them precisely is a cheap way to be taken seriously.

Writing about your research

Who is asking, and from where

  • Nobody arrives at a question from nowhere. Your training, your language, your funding and your access all shaped it before you noticed.
  • That is not a defect to apologize for. It is a condition to state, so the reader can weigh your account.
  • Ask yourself, in writing:
    • Why is this a question to me, and who else already treats it as one?
    • What can I see from here that an outsider cannot — and what is invisible from here?
    • Who benefits if my answer is the one that gets believed?
  • A paragraph, not a confession. Make it about the project.

What got counted, and what did not

  • Data is not found lying around. Someone decided to collect it, chose the categories, and paid for the collection.
  • Those decisions travel with the data forever:
    • A census category invented in 1890 still shapes what can be compared today.
    • An archive holds what an institution thought worth keeping, about the people it thought worth tracking.
    • A sensor network is dense where the money was and sparse where it was not.
  • Absence is evidence too. A population missing from the record is a finding about the record.
  • Read against the grain: ask what the document was made for, and you learn things it never meant to tell you.

Every dataset is a record of what someone decided was worth measuring.

Including yours.

Rigor is not neutrality

  • “All research is situated” is not a licence to say anything. It raises the standard rather than lowering it.
  • Rigor is what lets someone else check you:
    • Say what you did, in enough detail to be repeated or contested.
    • Show the evidence that cut against you, not only the evidence that fit.
    • State the limits of the claim before a reviewer does it for you.
    • Keep your sources traceable — a citation is a promise someone can follow.
  • Confidence comes from having tried to break your own argument, not from having avoided the attempt.

Questions to put to your own project

  • Framing — what does my question treat as given that could itself be studied?
  • Category — whose words am I using for the thing I am studying, and what do those words assume?
  • Voice — who is described in my project without getting to describe themselves?
  • Harm — who could be hurt by this being known, and by whom? Consent and anonymity are decisions, not forms.
  • Use — who will act on my findings, and would I be comfortable with how?
  • Falsification — what would convince me I am wrong? If nothing would, it is not research yet.

Where to Find Information — Public Data

  • United States, general
    • data.gov — federal catalog across every agency.
    • data.census.gov — census and American Community Survey, down to block group.
    • bls.gov/data — labor, wages, prices. wonder.cdc.gov — public health.
  • Environment and terrain
    • earthexplorer.usgs.gov and store.usgs.gov/map-locator — imagery, elevation, map products.
    • coast.noaa.gov/digitalcoast — climate, coastal, soils.
    • nass.usda.gov/AgCensus — agriculture, crops, producers.
  • International — data.worldbank.org, data.un.org, Eurostat.
  • Local government — most cities run a portal, e.g. NYC Open Data. Finest resolution, least documentation.
  • Download the codebook with the data. Read it before you plot anything.

Where to Find Information — Archives and Records

  • National Archives — archives.gov/research. Federal records, much of it digitized.
  • Library of Congress — loc.gov/collections. Manuscripts, maps, sound, photographs.
  • Newspapers — Chronicling America for pre-1963 US papers; your library likely licenses ProQuest for the rest.
  • Books at scale — HathiTrust, Internet Archive. Full-text search across millions of volumes.
  • Historical maps — David Rumsey Map Collection, USGS historical topographic maps.
  • Local and institutional archives — county historical societies, company records, university special collections. Email the archivist; they will tell you in one reply what a week of clicking will not.

Where to Find Information — Images, Scholarship, Other

  • Public domain images — Public Works, The Met open access, Public Domain Review, LoC pictures, Shorpy. Check the licence each time.
  • Scholarship — Google Scholar, JSTOR, Project MUSE, Semantic Scholar.
  • Patents — patents.google.com. How a technology was meant to work, drawn, at the moment it was new.
  • Standards, codes, regulations — often the only written record of what a practice was required to do.
  • People — practitioners, officials and residents hold what was never written down. An interview is a source; treat it with the same care as a document, and with more care about consent.

How to actually search

  • Find one good source, then work the citation graph:
    • Backwards — read its bibliography to find the conversation it came from.
    • Forwards — “cited by” in Scholar to find who answered it since.
    • Two or three passes and the recurring names are your field.
  • Find a recent review article or handbook chapter before you read twenty papers. Somebody has already mapped it.
  • Vary your vocabulary — different fields have different words for your object, and each word opens a different literature.
  • Use the library, not only the open web. Interlibrary loan gets you almost anything, free, in about a week.
  • Talk to the subject librarian. It is their job and almost nobody asks.
  • Keep a citation manager from day one. Zotero is free. Retrofitting a bibliography is misery.

Writing about your research

The winning proposal is always the clearest proposal that every reviewer understands.

Write in plain language, no jargon. Your readers are knowledgeable non-experts.

A general structure

  • Abstract / Summary
  • First Paragraph
  • Background and Previous Work
  • Objectives
  • Work Plan
  • Why It Matters — contribution and impact
  • Budget or Resources
  • Bibliography
Same skeleton for a grant, a thesis prospectus, or a conference abstract. Only the length changes.

Abstract, and the first paragraph

  • Abstract / Summary
    • Use plain language to say what you are going to do.
    • Save the background — it does not belong here.
    • Assume some readers read nothing else.
  • First paragraph
    • A hook sentence: the fact, the stake, or the puzzle.
    • Then, immediately: what are you going to do?
  • Write both last, after you know what the proposal actually says.

Background and Previous Work

  • Map the field you are working in — who is in this conversation, and what do they disagree about?
  • Identify the gap — what has not been asked, or has been asked only somewhere else, or was answered with evidence that has since changed.
  • Say why your work can address it — the gap and your method have to fit each other.
  • The gap is the hinge of the proposal. No gap, no reason to fund.
  • Do not manufacture one. “Nobody has studied this” usually means you have not found them yet, and a reviewer will know their names.

Objectives

  • Use verbs with clear aims — study x to understand y. Same sentence you wrote in Part II.
  • Be careful with “explore.”
    • You want the work to sound exploratory, not like a fishing trip.
    • The fix is a specific method, not a more confident adjective.
  • Three or four objectives. Eight reads as a project that has not decided what it is.
  • Each objective should be something you could later report as done or not done.

Work Plan

  • What are you going to do, in what order, by when? Explain it in plain, straightforward language.
  • Make it realistic. An over-promised plan reads as inexperience.
  • Tie each phase to an objective and to the evidence it produces.
  • Name the risk yourself — the archive may be closed, the sample may be small — and say what you will do then. Reviewers read that as command of the project.
  • Resources and budget — say what the work needs: access, time, equipment, people.
    • Pay yourself, so they know you will actually do the work. A budget with no salary line reads as a hobby.

Why It Matters — two different questions

  • Contribution to the field (what NSF calls intellectual merit)
    • How does the project contribute to the theory of x, or fill the gap of y?
    • Answer it in the reviewers’ own vocabulary, and point back to the gap you named.
  • Impact beyond the field (broader impact)
    • Societal, educational, environmental, economic, policy.
    • Who is the work for, and how will it reach them?
    • Equity and access should be intrinsic to the design of the project, not bolted on at the end. Reviewers can tell.
  • Research that stays private changes nothing. Say who the answer is for.

Bibliography

  • Show you know the field — and that you know how you can contribute to it.
  • It is read as evidence of competence, not as paperwork.
  • Cite the people you disagree with. Leaving them out looks like you missed them.

Pictures!

A timeline, a flow of data, a map of your sites. One clear diagram does the work of a paragraph.

Before next week

  • Write your project as one sentence: I will [verb] [material] in order to find out [what], because [who cares]. Make the question a how.
  • Find three sources and work each one backwards and forwards through its citations.
  • Name the one dataset or archive your project depends on, and confirm you can actually get it.
  • Name the framework you are already using, and write a paragraph on what it cannot see.
  • Write your field’s six answers — object, evidence, finding, tolerable error, where it goes, who judges it — and find two false friends between your field and the one closest to your project.
  • If the one-sentence version will not come out, that is the finding. Bring it and we will work on it together.
