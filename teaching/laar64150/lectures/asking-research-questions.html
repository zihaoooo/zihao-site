Your question is the design of your project.

Method, evidence, scope and reader are all downstream of it. Change the question and you have changed the whole project.

What a research question is for

  • It picks your method. A question about meaning needs interviews; a question about distribution needs data; a question about how something came to be needs an archive.
  • It picks your evidence. Once the question is fixed, you can name the documents, the dataset, the people, the site. If you cannot, the question is still too vague.
  • It picks your reader. It says which conversation you are joining, and therefore who is qualified to disagree with you.
  • It tells you when to stop. This is the one nobody mentions. A project without a question has no reason to stop collecting — so it sprawls until the deadline ends it.

Topic, question, hypothesis, thesis

What it isExampleWhen you have it
TopicA subject area. A noun.Urban heat.Day one.
QuestionSomething evidence can answer, that a reasonable person could answer differently.How does canopy loss track with heat complaints across a city’s districts?After the narrowing. This is the work.
HypothesisA stated expected answer, framed so it could turn out false.Districts that lost canopy since 2010 show higher heat complaints.Only when the field already has enough theory to predict.
ThesisThe answer you will argue, with the evidence behind it.Canopy loss tracks with complaints, but tenure, not tree count, explains who complains.At the end. Not the start.
A topic with a question mark on it is still a topic. A thesis submitted as a question means you have stopped looking.

Testing and revising

Ask a how question

  • When a project is stuck, the fix is nearly always the same: turn the question into a how.
  • Why asks for a cause. To answer it you have to rule out the other causes — which one researcher, in one semester, with one case, cannot do. So the answers come out either obvious or unprovable.
  • What asks for a description. You will get one, and then you will be sitting on a description wondering what the argument was.
  • How asks for a process — a sequence, a mechanism, an unfolding. A process has parts, and the parts are things you can go and look at.
  • Keep the why. It is your significance section, not your question.

Converting a why into a how

  • Why did the neighborhood gentrify?
    • → How did rezoning, lending and marketing work on each other to change who could afford to stay?
  • Why is the river polluted?
    • → How does the permitting system distribute discharge along the river, and who ends up downstream of whom?
  • Why don’t residents trust the agency?
    • → How do residents describe their encounters with the agency, and where in those accounts does the trust break?
  • Notice what happened: each rewrite named a process with parts, and each part is a place to go collect something.

What each question stem commits you to

StemYou are promisingEvidence it needsIts typical trap
What is / are…A description or a map.A sample, a survey, a catalog.Stops at description and never argues.
How does / did…A process or mechanism.A sequence you can trace: records, accounts, steps.Too many moving parts; bound it.
Why does…A cause.Comparison and control; ruling out rivals.Unanswerable at one project’s scale — unless your design can isolate.
Under what conditions…A boundary on a known claim.Cases that differ on one thing.Needs real contrast cases, not examples.
What is the relationship between…A correlation or a tension.Two things you can actually measure or read.Reporting correlation as cause.
Experimental work asks “what is the effect of” and answers it, because the design does the ruling-out. Match the stem to the evidence you can get.

The four dials

  • Place — where? A country, a watershed, a corridor, a building, a single room.
  • Time — when? A century, a decade, a season, the week of the storm.
  • Population or object — who or what, exactly? Which people, which documents, which species, which files.
  • Comparison — against what? Before and after, here and there, this group and that one. Comparison is the cheapest way to turn a description into an argument.
  • Turn them one at a time. Stop when you can picture the evidence — the actual folder, dataset or list of names.
  • You can over-narrow. A question about one block in one month has no one to talk to and nothing to compare.

One question, five drafts

  • 1. Housing. — A topic.
  • 2. Why is housing unaffordable? — A why, and one nobody could answer in a lifetime.
  • 3. How has housing become unaffordable in this city? — Better. A process, and a place. Still a century wide.
  • 4. How did conversions of rent-stabilized units change who lives in this district since 2010? — Place, time, population, and a process.
  • 5. How did conversions of rent-stabilized units change who lives in this district since 2010, compared with the district next to it that saw no conversions? — And now a comparison, which is where the argument comes from.
  • Nobody writes draft five first. You get there by writing draft two badly and looking at it.

The question and the gap have to fit each other

  • You found a gap in the literature. Your question has to be the right size for it.
  • Two symmetrical failures:
    • Gap too big for the question — “nobody understands urban heat, so I will interview six people.” The reader cannot see how you get from one to the other.
    • Gap too small for the question — a genuinely narrow hole in the literature, inflated into a claim about civilization. Reviewers read the inflation as inexperience.
  • The test: write the finding you hope to report, in one sentence. Does it close the gap you named? If not, one of the two has to move.

Testing and revising

FINER — a checklist for a draft question

  • Feasible — can you get the data, the access, the people, the time? Be brutal here.
  • Interesting — to you, and to somebody else. Name the somebody.
  • Novel — it confirms, extends, or contests something. It does not simply repeat it.
  • Ethical — it can be answered without harming the people it is about, and consent is possible.
  • Relevant — the answer changes something: a practice, a policy, a debate, a design.
  • Use it as a diagnostic, not a grade. No first draft scores well on all five. Find the weakest letter and fix that one.
  • It is usually F. Hulley et al., Designing Clinical Research

Borrow a framework to find the blank slot

  • PICO — Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome. Built for clinical questions, useful anywhere something is being done to something.
  • PEO — Population, Exposure, Outcome. For when nothing is being done on purpose — people are simply exposed to a condition.
  • SPIDER — Sample, Phenomenon of Interest, Design, Evaluation, Research type. The qualitative cousin, for questions about experience and meaning.
  • You do not have to write the letters into the proposal. Run your question through the slots and see which one you left blank.
  • It is usually the C — the comparison. Which is exactly the slot that would have made it an argument.

Seven ways a question fails

  • Too broad — no place, no time, no population. The dials are all at zero.
  • Too narrow — technically answerable, but nobody outside the case cares, and there is nothing to compare it to.
  • Already answered — and you would know, if you had worked the citation graph.
  • Unanswerable — the evidence does not exist, is sealed, or would take a decade and a team.
  • Smuggles the answer — how can we fix the failure of x? assumes the failure, names the villain, and fixes the finding in advance.
  • Not arguable — it has one obvious answer, or it is really a definition question.
  • Two questions in a trench coat — joined by an “and,” needing two methods, getting neither. Pick one; the other is your next project.

Your question will change. Say so.

  • You will revise it when the archive turns out to hold something else, when the data has a hole, when the first five interviews go somewhere you did not expect.
  • That is the method working. Evidence pushed back, and you listened.
  • What is not fine is the question moving silently — so the proposal answers one question and the final paper answers another, and neither reader can tell what happened.
  • Keep the drafts. Date them. A paragraph on how the question changed, and why, is one of the strongest things you can put in a methods section.
  • Say it out loud to someone who is not in your field. If they can repeat it back, it is clear. If they ask “so what?”, the relevance is not in it yet.

If you cannot name the evidence that would answer it, you do not have a question yet.

You have a topic, and a feeling about it.

Before next week

  • Write your question in three versions — one too broad, one you think is right, one too narrow. Bring all three.
  • Make at least one of them a how question.
  • Under each, write the one sentence of evidence that would answer it. Name the archive, the dataset, the people.
  • Run the middle one through FINER and mark the weakest letter.
  • Say it to someone outside your field and write down the first thing they ask you.
