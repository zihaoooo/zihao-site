Why it matters to you

The story Kuhn was arguing against

  • Science accumulates. Each generation adds facts to a growing pile. Error is removed, truth is retained, the pile gets taller.
  • Observation is neutral. Anyone with working eyes and honest habits sees the same data.
  • Theories are tested against that data and kept or discarded on the evidence, by a method that is the same everywhere.
  • Karl Popper had already complicated this: a theory is scientific if it can be falsified, and science advances by bold conjectures and their refutation.
  • Kuhn’s objection to Popper was historical. Scientists do not abandon a theory the first time it fails. If they did, no theory would ever survive its first week.

Where the book came from

  • Kuhn was a physicist. In 1947 he was asked to teach a science course for humanities students, and had to read Aristotle’s Physics.
  • Judged as mechanics, it was nonsense. Kuhn could not reconcile that with Aristotle being obviously brilliant about everything else.
  • The resolution: Aristotle was not doing bad Newtonian physics. He was asking different questions, of different objects, with different standards for what an answer looks like.
  • “Motion” did not mean for Aristotle what it means for Newton. Read as Newton, he is wrong. Read on his own terms, he is coherent.
  • That reading — take the past on its own terms — is a research method, and it is the one that produced the book.

Why it matters to you

Normal science

  • Most research, most of the time, is puzzle-solving inside an agreed framework. Kuhn calls this normal science, and he does not mean it as an insult.
  • A puzzle is a problem the framework guarantees has a solution. That guarantee is what lets a researcher commit years to a narrow problem.
  • Normal science is where the precision comes from. A community that has stopped arguing about foundations can go extremely deep.
  • The cost: normal science is not trying to falsify its own framework. It is trying to extend it, tidy it, and fit more of the world into it.
  • Almost everything any of us will do is normal science. That is fine. Know that it is what you are doing.

Anomaly

  • An anomaly is a result the framework says should not happen, and which will not go away.
  • Ordinary failures are absorbed all the time — bad instrument, bad sample, bad student. That is the correct first response, and usually right.
  • An anomaly is different because it persists, it shows up in more than one lab, and it sits somewhere the community cannot route around.
  • The response is not abandonment. It is patching: extra assumptions, corrections, special cases. The framework survives but gets baggier.
  • Kuhn’s point against Popper: this patching is not dishonesty. It is how research works, and it is usually the right call.

Crisis, and then revolution

  • Crisis is when the patches stop convincing people. The anomaly becomes the field’s central problem, and practitioners start arguing about foundations again.
  • In crisis, work gets philosophical, alternatives multiply, and the textbook consensus visibly loosens.
  • A paradigm is never abandoned into a vacuum. It is replaced. Until a rival exists, the community keeps the broken one, because a framework with problems beats no framework at all.
  • When the switch comes, Kuhn describes it as a gestalt switch — the same marks on the page, now read as a different figure. Afterwards the practitioners say they are working in a different world.
  • It is not a debate that someone wins on an afternoon. It is generational. Max Planck’s version: science advances one funeral at a time.

Why the textbook makes it look smooth

  • After a revolution, the textbooks are rewritten — and they are rewritten as though the new framework were where the field was heading all along.
  • Dead ends are cut. Losing arguments vanish. Predecessors get recruited as early versions of the winner.
  • So the discipline presents itself as a smooth accumulation, which is precisely the story Kuhn says is false.
  • This is why you had to be taught that science is cumulative: the textbook is written by the winners of the last revolution.
  • Your field does this too. Finding out what it edited out is a research project in itself.

Why it matters to you

“Paradigm” — a word Kuhn half regretted

  • In the 1962 text the word does too many jobs. Margaret Masterman counted twenty-one distinct senses of it, and Kuhn accepted that the criticism was fair.
  • In the 1969 postscript he split it in two:
    • Disciplinary matrix — everything a community shares: its symbolic generalizations, its models of what the world is made of, its values, its standards for a good solution.
    • Exemplar — the concrete worked problems students are drilled on. The pendulum, the inclined plane, the model organism, the canonical case.
  • The exemplar sense is the deeper one. You learn a paradigm by doing its problem sets, not by reading its axioms — which is why it is so hard to state and so hard to leave.
  • Late in life Kuhn said he would rather have used neither word than see “paradigm shift” become a marketing term.

What is actually in a paradigm

  • Ontology — what the field agrees the world is made of. Genes. Markets. Fields. Discourses. Sites.
  • Legitimate questions — which puzzles count as real problems, and which are dismissed as not our business.
  • Methods and instruments — what a competent person does, and what the equipment is assumed to be telling you.
  • Standards of a good answer — how much error is tolerable, what counts as proof, when you are allowed to stop.
  • Exemplars — the worked cases everyone was trained on, which supply the shape of every new problem.
  • Fill this in for your own field. Most people stall on the fourth row — and that is the one that causes trouble when they start working with another discipline.

Incommensurability

Why it matters to you

Incommensurability — no common measure

  • The word is borrowed from geometry. The diagonal of a square and its side are incommensurable: there is no unit that measures both a whole number of times.
  • They are perfectly comparable. You can say which is longer. There is just no common ruler.
  • Kuhn’s claim is that rival paradigms are like that. There is no neutral, framework-free standard that both sides accept, against which the contest could simply be decided.
  • Feyerabend arrived at a version of the same idea independently, and pushed it further.
  • So the disagreement is not only about the answers. It is about what the question is, what counts as evidence, and what a good answer would look like.

Three kinds, and the one Kuhn kept

  • Methodological — the two sides disagree about which problems matter and what counts as a solution, so they cannot even agree on the scoreboard.
  • Observational — trained differently, they do not see the same thing when they look. The expert sees a diagnosis where the novice sees a smudge.
  • Semantic, or taxonomic — the shared words do not mean the same thing. “Mass” in Newton and “mass” in Einstein are not interchangeable; the terms carve the world into different categories.
  • Later Kuhn narrowed the thesis to the third. Incommensurability became a claim about local failures of translation between the taxonomies of two theories, not a claim that scientists cannot understand each other.
  • That narrower claim is the defensible one, and it is the one to use.

What incommensurability does not mean

  • Not incomparability. Kuhn insisted the two can be compared — just not by a neutral algorithm that settles it.
  • Not unintelligibility. You can learn the other paradigm. Kuhn learned Aristotle’s. It takes work, like learning a language, and that work is exactly the point.
  • Not “anything goes.” Kuhn spent thirty years objecting to being read as a relativist, and he was right to.
  • Not progress-free. Kuhn thought later science solves more puzzles more precisely. What he denied was that it gets closer to a single true description of reality.
  • Davidson’s objection is the sharp one: to claim two schemes are untranslatable, you must already have translated enough to notice.

Kuhn-loss, and how a choice is still rational

  • Kuhn-loss: a new paradigm does not explain everything the old one did. Questions the old framework answered become unanswerable, or simply stop being questions. Revolutions are trades, not pure gains.
  • So how is the choice rational? In 1977 Kuhn named five shared values for judging a theory:
    • Accurate — agrees with what has been observed. Consistent — internally, and with other accepted theory.
    • Broad in scope — reaches past what it was built for. Simple — organizes what would otherwise be a pile.
    • Fruitful — produces new findings and new problems.
  • His move: these function as values, not rules. Everyone accepts them; they still do not settle the matter, because two reasonable people weigh them differently, and because they conflict — the more accurate theory is often the uglier one.
  • That is not a defect. A field where everyone was compelled to the same conclusion at the same moment could never change its mind.

Why it matters to you

You are inside one right now

  • Your field trained you into a matrix and a set of exemplars, and most of it was never stated out loud. It arrived through problem sets, studio reviews, lab protocols and what got a good grade.
  • You can catch it at the edges:
    • When a question strikes you as not a real question — that is the matrix, filtering.
    • When another field’s paper feels sloppy or obvious, and its authors are clearly not stupid.
    • When you cannot say why a piece of work is bad, only that it is.
  • None of that is a failing. It is what competence feels like from the inside.
  • But it means “that is not research” is usually a report about your training, not about the work in front of you.

Why interdisciplinary work is harder than it looks

  • An interdisciplinary project puts two paradigms in one room. The shared words are the dangerous part: “model,” “significant,” “bias,” “field” and “critical” mean different things on each side of the table.
  • Studies of interdisciplinary teams find the breakdowns are rarely logistical. They are disagreements about what counts as evidence, what a causal claim requires, and whether values belong in the analysis — and these come as self-reinforcing bundles.
  • Made worse by the institutions: separate journals, separate review standards, separate hiring lines, and funders who route work to one panel or the other.
  • The failure mode is predictable: the disagreement is never named, so it resurfaces later as a personality problem.
  • Name it in week one instead. “What would count as a convincing result for you?” is a cheap question with a high return.

Trading zones — how people work across it anyway

  • Peter Galison noticed that physicists and engineers from incompatible traditions did build radar and particle detectors together, without either side converting.
  • They built a trading zone: a local, limited arrangement where two communities exchange around a shared object, each keeping its own reasons for caring about it.
  • They develop an interlanguage — starting as jargon, thickening into a pidgin, occasionally becoming a creole a new field can be born in.
  • A boundary object is what makes this work: a drawing, a dataset, a prototype, a map — robust enough to be shared, loose enough that each side can read its own meaning into it.
  • The practical instruction: do not start by agreeing on the theory. Start by building the shared object, and let the vocabulary grow around it.

Three ways to misuse this lecture

  • Calling your project a paradigm shift. Kuhn’s revolutions are things a community does over a generation, not things a proposal announces about itself. Reviewers read the phrase as a tell.
  • Using incommensurability as an excuse. “We simply see the world differently” is where the conversation should start, not where it stops. The work is building the trading zone.
  • Reading it as “science is just opinion.” Kuhn denied this for thirty years. Paradigms are demanding, communities are disciplined, and the five values are real constraints — they just do not operate as an algorithm.
  • The honest takeaway is smaller and more useful: your standards came from somewhere, and other competent people have different ones.

“That is not research” is a sentence about your training.

It may also be true. But find out which one you are saying.

Before next week

  • Name three exemplars from your training — the worked cases you were drilled on that now supply the shape of every problem you see.
  • Write the five rows of your field’s matrix: what exists, which questions are legitimate, what the methods are, what a good answer looks like, which exemplars.
  • Find one anomaly in your field — a result people keep patching. Who is patching it, and with what?
  • Find one word that means something different in your field and in a field next door. Write both definitions.
  • Read Kuhn’s 1969 postscript. It is short, and it is him answering his critics.
