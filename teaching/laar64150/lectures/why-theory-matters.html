What is theory?

  • A well-substantiated explanation of an aspect of the natural world that can incorporate laws, hypotheses and facts. American Museum of Natural History
  • A supposition or system of ideas intended to explain something, especially one based on general principles independent of the thing to be explained. Oxford Dictionary
  • Notice these disagree. In the first, a theory has earned its standing through evidence. In the second, a theory may run ahead of its object, as a system of ideas.
  • Both senses are in daily use, in different buildings on the same campus. Know which one your reader has in mind.
  • Theory bridges, mediates and reconciles thought and action. Beth Meyer

Four things theory is not

  • Not a guess. The everyday “it is just a theory” is the opposite of the academic sense, where a theory is the most substantiated thing a field has.
  • Not a decoration. A famous name in your introduction that never touches your evidence again is not a framework. It is a citation.
  • Not the opposite of practice. Every practice runs on assumptions about how the world works. Theory is what those assumptions look like when written down.
  • Not optional. Refusing theory does not get you a view from nowhere — it gets you whatever assumptions your training installed, running unexamined.

What a theory actually does for you

  • It gives you vocabulary. Words you can argue with. Without them you are reinventing terms that a field settled forty years ago.
  • It tells you where to look. A framework nominates what is worth attending to — and by the same act, what to ignore.
  • It connects your case to other cases. This is how one study of one district becomes relevant to someone studying a different district.
  • It gives you something to disagree with. A finding that contradicts a framework is a contribution. A finding that contradicts nothing is a description.
  • Without a framework, a project is a very well-documented anecdote.

The stack beneath your method

  • Ontology — what you think exists. Is “a neighborhood” a real thing with properties, or a name people give to a pattern of behavior?
  • Epistemology — how you think it can be known. By measuring it? By asking the people in it? By reading what was written about it?
  • Methodology — the reasoned argument for why your methods suit that. The theory of your methods.
  • Method — the actual thing you do on Tuesday. The survey, the interview, the excavation, the model run.
  • Everyone has all four. Most people only ever choose the last one, and inherit the other three from their training without noticing.
  • A proposal is stronger when the stack lines up — and incoherent when it does not.

Find your discipline on this table. Then find the discipline next door. That gap is where most interdisciplinary arguments actually live.

Theoretical framework or conceptual framework?

  • Theoretical framework — you adopt an existing theory, largely whole, and work inside it. Your project inherits its vocabulary, its assumptions and its open problems.
  • Conceptual framework — you assemble the concepts this project needs, possibly from several sources, into a map of what you think relates to what.
  • Both are legitimate. Committees ask which one you are doing, and the wrong answer is not knowing.
  • A conceptual framework is often best drawn rather than written. Boxes, arrows, and what you expect to influence what.
  • Whichever you use, it should appear again in the analysis. A framework that shows up only in chapter two was decoration after all.

When theory goes wrong

Four things you can do with a theory

  • Apply it. Take the framework to a case it has not been taken to. Lowest risk, and a real contribution if the case is genuinely new.
  • Test it. Set up a case where the theory makes a prediction, and see. Requires the theory to be specific enough to be wrong.
  • Extend it. The theory nearly works; your case shows what has to be added for it to cover more ground.
  • Contest it. Your case is one the theory cannot handle, and you say why. The highest-value move, and the one students are most afraid to make.
  • You are allowed to contest a famous theory with a small case. That is, in fact, how most of them got revised.

How to choose one

  • Start from the question, not the reading list. A framework you picked because it was assigned will fight you for a year.
  • Match the grain. A theory pitched at the scale of civilizations will not explain what happened in one meeting; a theory of small-group interaction will not explain a century of policy.
  • Follow the citations. Whoever keeps appearing in the work closest to yours is offering you a framework and a conversation at once.
  • Read one primary source, not five summaries. Summaries hand you the vocabulary without the argument, and the argument is what you need.
  • Ask what it cannot see. Every framework has a blind spot. Naming yours in the proposal reads as command of the material.

The same evidence, two frameworks

  • Take one object: a highway cut through a neighborhood in 1961.
  • Through a framework of infrastructure and efficiency: the evidence is traffic counts, construction cost, travel times. The finding is about capacity, and the neighborhood is an input.
  • Through a framework of racial capitalism: the evidence is the route selection files, property valuations, who was compensated and how. The finding is about dispossession, and the traffic counts are an alibi.
  • Same highway. Same archive, even. Different framework, different finding.
  • Neither is a misreading. But there is no lens-free reading available, so state the one you are using.

Writing it into the proposal

  • One sentence, in this shape: I use [framework] because it treats [object] as [something], which lets me ask [question] of [evidence].
  • That sentence forces you to say what the theory does, not just that it exists.
  • Then, in the background section, place your framework in its conversation: who proposed it, who has contested it, where the argument stands.
  • Then, in the analysis, actually use its vocabulary. A reader should be able to see it working.
  • Then say what it does not reach, and what you would need to see it.

When theory goes wrong

Five ways theory goes wrong

  • Decoration — a famous name in the introduction, never seen again. The reader notices.
  • Alibi — theory used to excuse not gathering evidence. “My framework is critical, so I do not need data” is not a position, it is an exit.
  • Crushing — the framework is so strong that every case confirms it. If nothing could have come out otherwise, you ran an illustration, not a study.
  • Wrong grain — a theory of everything applied to a single afternoon, or a theory of one interaction stretched over a century.
  • Framework shopping — a whole semester auditioning theories instead of looking at anything. Pick one, use it on real evidence for two weeks, and you will learn more about whether it fits than from another month of reading.

A theory is an instrument, not an ornament.

It has a range, a resolution, and a blind spot. Know all three before you point it at something.

Before next week

  • Name the framework you are already using, whether or not you chose it. Write the sentence: I use [framework] because it treats [object] as [something].
  • Locate your discipline on the three positions table, and write one line on why it sits there.
  • Read one primary source of your framework — not a summary of it.
  • Write a paragraph on what your framework cannot see, and what evidence would fall outside it.
  • Decide which of the four moves you are making: apply, test, extend, or contest.
